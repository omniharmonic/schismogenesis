About and method

The AI Futures Fingerprint is part of Dialogos, a project by Benjamin Life. Its aim is not to sort people into teams. It is to help people who disagree about AI describe each other in terms the other side would sign.

Who is on the map

We read 554 public works by 127 people who shape how America argues about AI and society, in their own words: essays, speeches, testimony, letters and interviews with labelled speakers. They include lab leaders and engineers, safety researchers and whistleblowers, senators and governors from both parties, union leaders, economists, civil-rights scholars, critics of the attention economy, and religious voices. Each position needs enough on the record to compare: 88 people are shown, and the others will appear as more of their public work is read.

Where the statements come from

We drafted a pool of 51 statements: the 24 used before, plus 27 on AI and the social contract (jobs, wealth, power, children, truth, workers, rights). Two blind reviewers checked each new one for slant and for asking two things at once, and proposed the questions both lists had missed. Every person was then read on all 51.

The 27 statements you answer were chosen from that pool by a fixed rule. Each has people on both sides in their own words. Each helps tell the schools apart, reaches people not yet covered, and doesn't repeat a statement already chosen. There are nine in each part: what AI is, what matters and what to do. Statements nearly everyone agrees on were left out, because they can't show where you differ: 52 of 55 people on record want binding rules for frontier AI, for example.

How each person's positions were checked

Every position shown was read from the person's full texts, not from summaries or reputation. Two readers worked blind, each reading everything we have by that person. A position is shown only if two independent readings support it: both readers found it, or one did and a third reader, going back to the source, confirmed it. When the readings don't agree, we show nothing. Silence is never counted as disagreement.

The readers were told to watch for the mistakes that misrepresent people most: a warning read as an endorsement, an opponent's view the author is describing, irony, a hypothetical, a forecast read as a value, and a policy view read into a description of a problem. Every quote is checked by code, word for word, against the source. In this round, 1,039 positions passed across the pool. The two blind readers never once picked opposite sides on the same person and statement, and of the 426 positions from the earlier audit, 423 were found again and none came out on the other side.

Some sources turned out not to be the person's own words: a guest essay in a newsletter, a third party's summary of a podcast, a machine-written summary, a transcript where speakers can't be told apart, or a leaked private meeting. Those are excluded entirely.

How you're compared

For each person we count the statements where you both took a side, and how many you agree on. A person needs at least three shared statements to be ranked, and the score is smoothed so that one of one doesn't beat nine of eleven. Your neighbourhood is the school of your nearest people.

Camps, schools and close circles

The groups come from the people we have read most fully, 56 of them, compared on the survey's statements and on the other ideas in the atlas they took a side on. Agreement counts only statements both people took a side on, and each statement's weight is divided by how much it overlaps the others, so one question asked five ways doesn't get five votes.

We then redraw the groups 300 times, each time from a resampled set of statements and with a different number of groups, and count how often each pair of people lands together. The groups are read off those counts at four depths: camps, whose members land together at least a quarter of the time; schools inside them (42%); branches (60%); and close circles who land together at least 78% of the time. Each group shows its own number. Where a group doesn't split further, there isn't enough evidence yet, not necessarily agreement.

Today that gives 4 camps, 11 schools, 11 branches and 2 close circles. People read less fully are placed down the tree only as far as they clearly agree with one group more than the others. Someone close to two camps is shown between them. If you answer as any grouped person did, your nearest match is in their own camp 89% of the time and in their own school 70% of the time: the finer the group, the more statements it takes to tell it apart.

The live room

In the room, opinion groups are found the way Polis finds them: principal components of the votes, small base clusters, then two to five groups chosen by how cleanly they separate, checked by resampling. Bridging statements are found the way Community Notes ranks notes: a model that separates how much people agree with a statement from how much that agreement depends on which side they're on. A bridge is something people on both sides agree with.

Disclosures

  • The reading of public work was done with Claude, a model made by Anthropic. People on the map with ties to Anthropic: Dario Amodei and Jack Clark (co-founders), Jan Leike (works there), Anton Korinek (an unpaid seat on its economic advisory council), Divya Siddarth (her Collective Intelligence Project has worked with it). Nvidia, Jensen Huang's company, has invested in Anthropic. California's government, under Gavin Newsom, uses Anthropic tools, and Anthropic backed Scott Wiener's SB 53.
  • Other industry ties: Jaron Lanier and Kate Crawford work at Microsoft Research, Dean Ball leads a team at OpenAI, and Michael Kratsios worked at Scale AI.
  • Statements from season one keep their reviewed wording. New statements were reworded where both blind reviewers found slant, and the original drafts are kept in the project's records.

What this can't tell you

  • None of the thinkers has reviewed how they are read here. Each is read from a handful of works, and some from very few.
  • Some thinkers have too few stated positions on these statements to compare. Your result names them.
  • Twenty-seven statements and 88 people show a pattern, not a population. Some questions people care about, such as legal liability for AI companies or protecting whistleblowers, are left out because no one on the record takes the other side yet.

If you think a thinker is misread, use “Report a misreading” on your result. Every report is answered.
