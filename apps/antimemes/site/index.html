An arts lab for dialogue across difference

You didn't pick your enemies. You copied them.

antimemes.is builds instruments for seeing the culture you're inside, meeting the people on the far side of it, and getting credit for understanding them. Not for winning.

Desire is borrowed.

René Girard's starting point: we rarely want things on our own. We want what a model wants. When the model is close enough to want the same thing, the model becomes a rival, and the rivalry spreads by imitation too. Online, everyone is close.

>it isn't even mine

Then the room splits in two.

Gregory Bateson called it schismogenesis. In the symmetrical kind, each side answers with more of the same: boast for boast, outrage for outrage. In the complementary kind, each move invites its opposite: one side leads, so the other follows, so the first leads harder. Either way the gap widens every round, until the difference is all either side has left.

Feeds run the symmetrical kind at scale. Sort everyone by one question and the middle disappears.

And every crisis wants a victim.

When rivals have copied each other until they're doubles, the crisis has no clean exit. Girard's account of how groups end it: all against one. The crowd unites against a victim, and the calm that follows gets credited to the victim's removal. He called it the scapegoat mechanism. Online it never finishes. There is a new one every morning.

On the map, every face turns toward one node. It has no face of its own.

The usual fixes feed the machine.

Every move below is a reasonable thing to do. Each one hands the rivalry a new object.

  1. Debate them

    Now there's a winner and a loser, and a highlight reel of the loss.

  2. Fact-check them

    Now there's a referee, and both sides can fight the referee.

  3. Post more good content

    More models to imitate, faster. The feed doesn't care which side you're on.

  4. Dunk on the people dunking

    Congratulations, you found today's scapegoat.

  5. Stop fighting over the same object

    Give people something to want that the other side has to grant, and that never runs out.

An antimeme is an idea you can't copy. You have to meet it.

A take spreads in a second. Understanding someone doesn't spread at all: it has to be done again, by each person, each time. That's the opening this lab works in. We call it anti-mimesis: meeting difference without imitating it or fighting it.

Three theories, one instrument.

Girard: mimetic desire

Desire is learned from models, so people close to each other end up wanting the same things and turn into rivals, then into each other's doubles. Crowds have settled the crisis by turning on one victim. We take the diagnosis and refuse the remedy: an order that needs an enemy is the machine, not the cure.

Bateson: schismogenesis

The split is a feedback loop, not a flaw in anyone's character. Symmetrical: each side answers with more of the same. Complementary: each move calls up its opposite. Bateson also asked what holds such loops in check. Our answer is to change what a move is rewarded for.

Habermas: communicative action

Strategic action moves people by pressure or price. Communicative action moves them by reaching understanding: every claim promises it is true, right and sincerely meant, and has to make good on that with reasons when challenged, until only the force of the better argument decides. Its ground is the lifeworld, the shared background we all talk from. Feeds run on strategic action. We build a small space for reasons.

How we know it's working

Not agreement. Meta-consensus.

John Dryzek and Simon Niemeyer use the term for agreement on what a disagreement is about: which values are at stake, which facts are in dispute, which options are real, without agreeing on the answer. Our test is a strict version of it. Every group can describe every other group in words the described side would sign.

Three instruments.

Your answers to The Great AI Debate are the first move in all three. The first map is AI policy. The instruments are built so the map can grow, one debate at a time, to the wider pop-culture memetic landscape.

SeeLive

Map the landscape from people's own words

Public figures are placed by what they've said on the record. Every claim is tied to its exact words and a link to the source, checked in code. Groups are found in the data first and named afterwards.

Then your answers place you on the same map. If you choose to add them, they're counted anonymously in the crowd shown beside the thinkers, so you can see where the public and the people who argue about AI for a living split.

MeetLive: the room

Write for the far side

In the live room you write statements and vote on other people's. The room finds its own groups from the votes. A statement counts as a bridge only when people on both sides of a split agree with it, so pleasing your own side earns nothing.

Next: anti-debates. Nobody rebuts until they can state the other side's case so well the other side agrees.

  1. A states B's case at its strongest.
  2. B says "yes, that's us", or corrects it.
  3. Only then does A disagree.

A clean, mutually understood disagreement counts as a result.

MoveNext

Reputation for being understood across

Like Community Notes, pointed across groups. A description of a group earns two things: fidelity, which only that group can grant by signing it, and portability, when outsiders find it clear. Reputation follows signed descriptions, not followers.

        Rules of engagement

        • Public figures, public words

          People are placed only from what they've published, and every placement can be disputed.

        • Nobody private

          We infer nothing about private people. Your answers stay in your browser unless you choose to add them. Then they're sent under a random ID, and no group smaller than ten is ever shown. Privacy

        • Short quotes, always linked

          We quote a sentence or two and link to the source. Full transcripts are never republished.

        • Agents never ratify

          Software can draft and assist. Only people in the described group can sign a description of it.

        • No verdicts

          We don't score who's right. We score whether groups can describe each other.

        • Adults only

          The survey is for people 18 and over.
