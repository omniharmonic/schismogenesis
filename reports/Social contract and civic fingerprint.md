# Keep Answers Private, Make Understanding Public

The five pieces fit together only if they stay separate, because each answers a different question. The memetic map shows what public thinkers argue and why. A Polis-style round shows what divides a particular group of people for about a month. A civic fingerprint gives one person a private, item-by-item picture of where they stand. Community Notes-style bridging tests whether a piece of writing survives across difference. An extended dialogue on a renewed social contract is the staged public process that uses all four, and its honest product is a set of descriptions each side has signed, not a contract. Polis clustering cannot carry more than that: most participants vote once under a cookie, its group count is largely a platform output, and groups from two conversations have no defined relation. BLOOM, Declare Interdependence and Declaring Our Tech Futures are building assemblies and coalition, while nobody found so far measures whether groups can describe each other, so the opening is a small measurement role, not another assembly. A fixed question set beats extraction for comparing people and loses to it for discovery, so it is an added layer and not a replacement. The sharpest new finding is about risk: about 40 answers single out almost everyone, even with three answer options, so a stored answer vector is a stable key and can never honestly be called anonymous. That collides with what is already specified, because ratification, audits and the viewpoint model all rely on reactions held on the server. Nothing built was changed, and every recommendation here is an option for Benjamin's decision. The nearest dates are the 20 November BLOOM host deadline, where Dialogos is a weak fit as an applicant, and the decisions on eligibility and deck size due 6 December.

This report is evidence for a separate vision document, so it concentrates on evidence, tensions and contradictions. Judgments labeled "my reading" are inference. Most 2025 and 2026 papers cited are unreviewed preprints, several were seen only as abstracts or search summaries, and web pages were read through a summarizing tool, so anything quoted publicly should be reopened first. Three tests below (uniqueness, cluster inference, reliability arithmetic) were run for this research on public data and are illustrative, not peer reviewed. This is not legal advice. One disclosure: this report was written by an Anthropic model, and Anthropic's proposals are among those assessed.

## Five tools answer five questions, and merging them breaks each one

The table is the shortest answer to how the pieces fit. The right-hand column matters most, because every failure found in this research came from a tool being asked to claim more than it measures.

| Layer | Question it answers | Whose data | Who should hold it | What it must not claim |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Memetic map | What do public thinkers hold, and why? | Quote-anchored public works | Public | That thinkers' camps are the public's camps |
| Polis-style round | What divides this group of people right now? | One conversation's votes | The round's organizers, aggregates only | That groups persist or compare across rounds |
| Civic fingerprint | Where do I stand, and how sure is that? | One person's answers | That person, on their device | That it is an identity, a type, or a sample of the public |
| Bridging score | Does this text survive across difference? | Ratings on specific texts | Pseudonymous, with a sunset | That survival means truth or agreement |
| Signing and prediction scores | Can A describe B in words B signs, and does A know what B thinks? | Ratings by B, votes by B | Published per cluster pair | That this is meta-consensus as a whole, or consent to policy |
| Extended dialogue | Could these parties fairly negotiate, and what would they need to understand first? | All of the above, staged | Public process with rules published in advance | That it has a mandate or has produced a contract |

The strongest sequence in the evidence runs breadth, then depth, then breadth. Ireland went assembly, parliament, binding referendum, and vTaiwan went topic definition, Polis, live meeting, draft ([Bertelsmann](https://www.bertelsmann-stiftung.de/en/our-projects/democracy-and-participation-in-europe/shortcut-archive/shortcut-8-trailblazers-of-digital-participation-taiwans-join-platform-and-vtaiwan)). No controlled study compares sequences, so this rests on observation alone. My reading of how Dialogos's parts would slot in: the map supplies seed statements and the real quotes used for calibration; an open round lets people add what thinkers missed; small balanced groups write descriptions of each other; a second broad round signs or corrects them; the bridging score tests what travels. The fingerprint stays outside this chain as a personal tool, and shares only anchor questions with it.

### Polis finds a month's disagreement and cannot give a lasting public

Fifteen limits were checked. Nine have direct evidence in code or in Gelauff's study of 119 conversations, three rest on inference from the design, and three have no direct study at all. The deepest one cannot be fixed by better math.

| Limit | Evidence | What would address it |
|---|---|---|
| No lasting identity | **89.1% of participants vote in one session**, and the median first session is 30 votes in 239 seconds. A participant is a cookie ([Gelauff](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.28368)) | Shared anchor items and opt-in pseudonyms, not a new algorithm |
| Group count is partly an artifact | 74.6% of conversations report two groups, 62.5% on recomputation, and the two agree in 70 of 112 cases. Exported clusters cannot be reproduced ([Gelauff](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.28368)) | Frozen snapshots with full recompute |
| Consensus fails on thin data | The group-informed consensus score turns negatively correlated with the true ranking at low observation rates, and the median real rate is 15.5% ([Blair et al.](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~nisarg/papers/bridging.pdf)) | Averaging over many splits, not one partition |
| Conveners shape the result | Median 23 seeds. Votes on statements later rejected are zeroed: 180,944 votes, 89% real ([Gelauff](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.28368)) | Moderation frozen before scoring |
| Pass hides several states | Rejected statements drew 9.3 points less agreement and 6.7 points more passes, but not significantly more disagreement, so pass is doing the work of "this statement is bad" ([Gelauff](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.28368)). Pass also dilutes rates in the code ([repness.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/compdemocracy/polis/edge/delphi/polismath/pca_kmeans_rep/repness.py)) | Named non-answers |
| Silence is not neutral | People take 10.1% longer when disagreeing with the majority, in 153 of 155 conversations ([Gelauff](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.28368)), and routing depends on prior votes | Missingness models, plus logged exposure |
| Some votes are not positions | Authors get an automatic agree, and 18.5% of 6,980 scorable statements had a predicted stance of disagreement ([Gelauff](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.28368)) | Ask authors to vote like everyone else |
| Thin per-person data | vTaiwan Uber: about 10.57 votes per person on 197 comments ([Halpern et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.15608)) | Adaptive selection, which is required for fair representation at realistic vote counts ([same](https://arxiv.org/html/2211.15608v4)) |
| A cluster is not one voice | Group output is one label per person, with no uncertainty. Mixed-membership models and proportional slates exist ([Fish et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.01291v3)) | Soft membership, and a slate where every sizable faction has a statement it can sign |

Three limits remain unstudied: whether statement order changes votes, whether anyone can vote strategically to bend clusters, and whether Polis groups match survey-validated groups. Almost no replacement method has been published on Polis data. The closest tests are on legislative votes, and public exports ([compdemocracy/openData](https://github.com/compdemocracy/openData), [valency-anndata](https://patcon.github.io/valency-anndata/api/datasets)) would let any of them run offline at no cost. Bayesian multidimensional models, co-clustering with informative missingness, and mixed membership are the suggested order. Tensor methods are not worth pursuing now, since a person-by-statement-by-time table is mostly disconnected slices when almost nobody returns.

Extended use changes the picture further. The largest open US case, Bowling Green's What Could BG Be, drew **7,890 people, 3,940 ideas and 1,034,868 votes in 33 days**, roughly 1 in 10 local residents ([StateScoop](https://statescoop.com/bowling-green-kentucky-ai-community-planning/); [Apolitical, promotional](https://apolitical.co/en/navigator/case-studies/using-ai-to-make-sense-of-nearly-8000-residents-ideas-for-a-25-year-community-plan)). It had a county sponsor, a local firm and Google.org ([project page](https://www.whatcouldbgbe.com/about-the-project)), its representativeness is unevaluated, and over half its ideas reached 80% agreement. That pattern fits a local agenda, and foundational questions should not be sold as if they would behave the same way. Its eight seeded themes also missed K-12 education until the analysis tool surfaced it ([GovTech](https://www.govtech.com/artificial-intelligence/bowling-green-ky-used-ai-to-drive-public-engagement)). My reading is that Polis is a one-month instrument, and every multi-month case got its length from staged programmes with replaced members, not from sustained activity on one tool.

### Long dialogues die at the handoff to power, not for lack of voices

No case failed for lack of participation or a poor text alone. Iceland's draft won 66.9% in an advisory referendum ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_Icelandic_constitutional_referendum)), 32 of 63 MPs backed it, and it never reached a vote ([openDemocracy, written by a supporter](https://www.opendemocracy.net/en/democracy-on-ice-post-mortem-of-icelandic-constitution/)). Chile failed twice from opposite sides. Rejection reached 61.87% in 2022 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2022_Chilean_constitutional_referendum)) and 55.76% in 2023 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2023_Chilean_constitutional_referendum)), with each drafting body read as one side's. France promised to pass climate proposals "sans filtre" and rejected 3 of 149 ([IDDRI](https://www.iddri.org/fr/print/pdf/node/25644)), with many others softened ([ASFE](https://alliancesolidaire.org/2026/04/08/la-convention-citoyenne-pour-le-climat-une-ambition-democratique-au-bilan-contraste/)). Co-creator Jason Hsu says vTaiwan has had no major decisions since 2018, partly because it is not binding ([syndicated report](https://ca.news.yahoo.com/taiwan-tried-digitize-democracy-huge-084810185.html)). The Grand Débat's self-selected participants mostly did not support the Yellow Vests who triggered it ([Monnery and Wolff, abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/p/hal/journl/hal-04197291.html)). Ireland, the clearest success, cost €2,355,557 to March 2019 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citizens%27_Assembly_(Ireland))) and had a state mandate plus a binding vote. Chile is the main warning for this project, because a process owned by one cluster reproduces the divide and a wider public then rejects it.

Mechanics matter for a tiny team. Random invitation yields about 5 to 6% acceptance and still skews ([Mehr Demokratie, secondary](https://www.mehr-demokratie.de/en/mehr-wissen/citizens-assemblies/news/details-view/a-book-and-its-consequences)). Dropout is not neutral: in Finland, negative views on immigration and reluctance to meet opposing opinions predicted leaving an immigration deliberation ([Karjalainen and Rapeli, abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/qualqt/v49y2015i1p407-422.html)). Marathon County's two-year panel balanced groups of about 10 at 3 liberal, 4 moderate and 3 conservative, paid a $100 gift card, and still saw conservatives fall from 28% enrolled to 23% attending ([Let's Talk report, organizer's own](https://wipps.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Lets-Talk-Key-Findings-Elections-Dialogues-Final-Report-1.8.2025.pdf)). A Cornish panel ran for just under £1,500 against typical quotes of £25,000 to £60,000, and its organizers warned of a "talking shop" ([Citizen Network](https://citizen-network.org/library/learning-from-penzance-citizens-panel.html)). **No case shows a volunteer team holding a national, cross-divide dialogue for months, and no case shows reputation alone keeping people.** Treat that as an untested bet. America in One Room's large policy and affect shifts mostly reverted within a year, leaving more civic engagement among low-interest participants ([summary](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/America_in_One_Room); [Hoover](https://www.hoover.org/plus/research/can-deliberative-discussions-heal-political-divides)), so opinion change is a fragile headline anyway.

A minimum version was sketched for about 20 weeks of part-time work: one question rather than "the social contract"; four weeks seeding, with critics in each cluster reviewing the seeds; about 30 days of open round with a public "who is missing" gauge; small rotating groups writing each other's positions; a second broad round on those descriptions; and a report of disputes with named dissent. Paying $100 per round to 30 to 40 people is $3,000 to $4,000 per round, above the near-zero budget, so that choice needs Benjamin's approval. Every one of those steps is outward-facing and nothing should start without his sign-off.

### A signed description can come before a contract, and cannot replace one

Benjamin has a real argument, and it is best stated plainly in paraphrase. A contract that excluded most parties cannot be walked away from, because nobody can exit a shared place, so it must be renegotiated with those left out, by a consent that includes refusal (`wiki/writing/the-law-of-the-land`, a private draft, not for circulation). AI removes the leverage labor held, which he reads as dissolving the old bargain (`wiki/writing/the-solarpunk-revolution-is-inevitable`). My reading is that these rest on two different theories. One says rights exist because labor was needed. The other says people are owed consent because they are embedded and affected. **Only the second survives AI**, since a person who is no longer economically needed has no claim under the first. This is the most useful thing to make explicit in the vision document. His wording is "renegotiate" and "refounding"; the exact phrase "renewed social contract" was not found in what was read.

The theory traditions sort into three families: bargain (Hobbes, Gauthier), justification (Rawls, Scanlon, Habermas) and relationship (Tully, treaty and covenant traditions). His latest work sits in the third. Scanlon's test, whether a principle could be reasonably rejected ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/contractualism/)), is the closest match to "the described side would sign." That phrase has its own problem. Hume and Dworkin objected that inherited or hypothetical consent binds no one ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/contractarianism/)), so "would sign" is not consent. Only actual signing counts, and any prediction must be labeled as a prediction.

A map can supply a contract's preconditions: who the parties are, who is missing, what is disputed, and whether each side has been fairly described. It cannot supply terms, consent or enforcement. So the compatible order has three stages: **recognition** (each side can describe the other in words it signs), **meta-consensus proper** (agreement on which values are legitimate, which beliefs credible, what the options are), and **terms** (a real agreement by people with a mandate). Dryzek and Niemeyer's definitions support that order ([AJPS](https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/13db0edd-bc9e-45ab-ae40-9194d14bd78a/content)), and they warn that meta-consensus can be manipulated. The risk to name is understanding used as the respectable face of terms set elsewhere.

"Social contract" works better as a contested topic than as the product's promise. The phrase went mainstream through OpenAI's April 2026 paper, and critics call it a settlement written by the party that benefits most ([Hybrid Horizons](https://hybridhorizons.substack.com/p/the-social-contract-openai-wrote); [Fortune](https://www.fortune.com/2026/04/06/sam-altman-says-ai-superintelligence-is-so-big-that-we-need-a-new-deal-critics-say-openais-policy-ideas-are-a-cover-for-regulatory-nihilism)). A useful sorting question for any proposal: does it change who decides, or only how the gains are shared? Most lab proposals do the second ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-policy-responses); [Noema](https://www.noemamag.com/openai-proposes-a-social-contract-for-the-intelligence-age/)). The pressure for sharing power comes from unions ([SHRM on the AFL-CIO](https://www.shrm.org/advocacy/afl-cio-launches-workers-first-initiative-on-ai)) and from Pope Leo XIV, who writes that a more moral AI is not enough if a few determine that morality ([*Magnifica Humanitas*](https://www.vatican.va/content/leo-xiv/en/encyclicals/documents/20260515-magnifica-humanitas.html)). Rival frames imply different parties: contract, treaty, covenant, refounding, New Deal, commons, civilizational race, exit. Which clusters accept which is an untested hypothesis. Recorded evidence from his own scene shows one word warming some people and repelling others (`wiki/writing/the-law-of-the-land`). Frames could become objects on the map, with their implied parties, implied outcome and who rejects them, plus a "refuse the frame" option in every encounter.

One possible public sentence, for him to judge: Dialogos does not write the new contract. It helps people find out whether they could fairly negotiate one, and what they would have to understand about each other first. Three cautions travel with it. His own contract argument belongs in signed essays and not in the map's default language, or the map carries his view as the summit. Two claims in his essays, that nearly everyone wants the same thing and that elites are feudal, would likely fail his own signing test and should be tested on the map. Treaty and Haudenosaunee concepts should follow his own rule of asking before borrowing.

## A fixed question set wins comparison and loses discovery

The direct answer to whether a fixed set can outperform whole-internet extraction by tracking a few voices and questions: it outperforms on comparing people, cost and placing a person, and it underperforms on finding what you did not think to ask. The comparison also rests on a false premise, because Dialogos is not extracting the whole internet. It already reads a watchlist. The real choice is what sits on top of that watchlist.

The case for fixed items is strong and concrete. Current data has about 10% fill, so about 2.7 stances per idea (my arithmetic from `packages/arena_map/src/arena_map/structure.py`), and only 80 of 734 machine-proposed holdings survived blind reading (`docs/adr/ADR-035-idea-families-and-two-reader-structure.md`). People who speak to different things cannot be compared. The measurement literature adds that single answers are noisy while averages across items are stable, with stability approaching party identification as items rise ([Ansolabehere et al.](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/strength-of-issues-using-multiple-measures-to-gauge-preference-stability-ideological-constraint-and-issue-voting/16F6AF97F7B71AA0112EC9ADF78B553A)). Pew's 2026 typology sorts people into nine groups from 30 questions, n = 10,357 ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2026/06/10/beyond-red-vs-blue-the-political-typology/)).

The case against is equally strong. **Whoever picks the items draws the map.** Across 500,000 configurations of 36 statements from a list of 50, one Belgian party received between 5% and 32% of advice ([Walgrave et al.](https://medialibrary.uantwerpen.be/oldcontent/container2608/files/Walgrave%20et%20al%202009%20-%20voting%20aid%20applications.pdf)), and only 43% of Dutch users of two tools got identical advice (same source). Drawing items from elite disagreement bakes in the agenda problem Bachrach and Baratz named, where some issues are organised out of politics ([Sage](https://sk.sagepub.com/ency/edvol/embed/power/chpt/second-face)). A study of 4.7 million AI tweets found communities of use, with none that discussed AI as such ([Kaiser and Green](https://firstmonday.org/ojs/index.php/fm/article/download/14436/12423)), so thinkers' camps are likely not the public's camps. Sophisticated people are more constrained: one dimension explains about twice as much among high-knowledge respondents ([Lauderdale, Hanretty and Vivyan](https://chrishanretty.co.uk/pubs/10.1086_695673/index.html)), so axes learned from elites will fit the public worse.

"A few key voices" also needs correcting. The best early-warning evidence is about network-central people, not famous ones: followees of random users adopted widespread hashtags a mean 7.1 days before controls ([Friends as Sensors](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1211.6512)). In the best US study of who leads, legislators were more likely to follow than lead public discussion ([Barberá et al.](https://csmapnyu.org/research/who-leads-who-follows-measuring-issue-attention-and-agenda-setting-by-legislators-and-the-mass-public-using-social-media-data)). Simulations also find large shifts driven mostly by easily influenced people influencing each other ([Watts and Dodds](https://pdodds.w3.uvm.edu/research/papers/years/2007/watts2007a.pdf)). So a watchlist is sound for one job and unproven for another. It detects **new forks among those voices**, nearly by construction. It does not detect **new questions in the public**. No study was found testing whether intellectuals, as opposed to legislators, lead opinion.

The spec also matters here. It chose "full claim extraction and connection, not question-first scoring" (`docs/01-BUILD-SPEC-v1-SLICE-1.md`), and D-31.4 rejected "a fixed proposition bank scored by a cheap classifier" because accuracy matters more than cost at this scale. A fixed set therefore needs a decision by Benjamin. My reading is that the rejection covered a cheap classifier replacing extraction, for Slice 1. It did not consider a small tested layer on top of extraction, with people answering and careful reading of thinkers. But that is an interpretation of a locked document, so I flag it and do not resolve it.

| Layer | Job | Starting size (proposals, not validated) | Weakness to report |
|---|---|---|---|
| Claim-first extraction on the watchlist | Discovery, evidence, quotes | As specified | Sparse, so people cannot be compared |
| Anchor set of tested statements | Comparing people and rounds | 40 to 60, of which at least a quarter public-origin (a judgment, no source) | Item choice is political, so publish the rules and resample |
| Trigger for new questions | Catching forks cheaply | A new proposition draws opposed stances from two or more clusters in a short window | Detects elite forks only. Backtest on past months first |
| Sensor layer | Early signal beyond fame | Central ordinary sources, counted at topic level, never profiling individuals | Hashtags show adoption, not opinion. Platform terms unchecked |

Four tests would decide this offline or with a pilot. Resample the anchor set many times and publish how often a person's nearest school changes. Measure how much public disagreement lies outside the directions thinkers span, an "elite-blind share" that should be a headline number. Check whether placement predicts a person's unseen answers better than party alone. Replay past months with the date frozen to measure lead time and false alarms.

### Thinkers should be placed into the public's space, which reverses the current plan

Current design has landmarks pre-populate the space so a newcomer lands on existing terrain (`docs/04-RESEARCH-SYNTHESIS.md`, S5). The elite-public scaling evidence points the other way. Jessee shows the standard strategy of scaling citizens and elites together can go wrong ([abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/amposc/v60y2016i4p1108-1124.html)), and Shiraito, Lo and Olivella find a sizeable share of voters read shared items differently from legislators ([abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.05934)). With 27 thinkers, there are too few respondents to calibrate items at all. The better-supported route is to calibrate on the public, then score thinkers under those fixed settings, with intervals. No validated minimum bridge count exists. The best guidance is above 20% of items when misfit is present ([Fischer et al., abstract](https://doaj.org/article/53599c6075af4f7daeadbd0b5c7e8e86)), and one Swiss study used 7 of 51 ([Walder](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpos.2024.1425406/full)). Machine-coded stances can act as responses in form ([Burnham, preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.02472)), but **nobody has tested text-coded thinker stances against the same public's answers on the same items**. Accuracy also collapses within a camp: correlation with a roll-call benchmark was 0.967 overall and 0.649 among Republicans ([Wu et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.12057)). The 27 thinkers are mostly a within-camp problem.

The practical cost is a calibration sample, proposed at about 1,000 opted-in adults, which is a judgment with no source behind it. That sample cannot come from an open web page. An LLM survey-taker reportedly evaded detection 99.8% of the time at about $0.05 per response ([404 Media on Westwood](https://www.404media.co/a-researcher-made-an-ai-that-completely-breaks-the-online-surveys-scientists-rely-on/)), though the paper itself was not read. Taiwan recruited by text message to random numbers ([Tang transcript](https://archive.tw/speech/63852928)). So calibration needs recruited people, and that is spending Benjamin would have to approve. Self-selected data is not useless: effects among over 50,000 voting-advice users correlated r = 0.65 with a probability sample, with AfD voters at 5.5% against 19.8% ([Thomeczek, abstract](https://www.cogitatiopress.com/politicsandgovernance/article/view/11241)). That helps with relationships between answers. It does not help with claims about how many people hold a view.

The current code cannot yet place a new person. `person_space` treats silence as zero and then shifts it, centres on the mean of the 27 thinkers so everyone would read as one-sided, and the layout positions only those fitted. Unanswered items would need to count as missing, the centre would need a public reference, and the layout would need a way to add a point and stay pinned per release (`packages/arena_map/src/arena_map/structure.py`). These are noted for awareness and nothing has been changed.

## The civic fingerprint is buildable, useful, and dangerous to store

A fingerprint is measurable, with limits. The best decomposition of US issue opinion gives one left-right axis about 1/7 of the variation, real personal idiosyncrasy about 3/7, and instability about 3/7 ([Lauderdale, Hanretty and Vivyan](https://chrishanretty.co.uk/pubs/10.1086_695673/index.html)). That supports Benjamin's instinct, provided the fingerprint keeps the idiosyncratic part and does not flatten people onto axes. Another classification calls 72.8% ideological, 20.7% idiosyncratic and 6.5% inattentive ([Fowler et al.](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/usappblog/2022/10/19/most-americans-are-ideologically-moderate)). The two need not conflict, since one is a share of variance and the other a label on people, and a published response disputes the second's method. Types are a labeled convenience: dimensional findings outnumber categorical ones about 5 to 1 across psychology ([Haslam et al.](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/dimensions-over-categories-a-meta-analysis-of-taxometric-research/)), and political type counts run 3, 6, 7 and 9 depending on method ([Baldassarri and Goldberg](https://typeset.io/pdf/neither-ideologues-nor-agnostics-alternative-voters-belief-4bm2749t1h.pdf); [Feldman and Johnston](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/pops.12055); [Hidden Tribes summary](https://www.ilctr.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/More-in-Common-Americas-Hidden-Tribes.pdf); Pew, above). No published shelf life exists for a fingerprint, and a yearly retest with items re-dated more often is a working assumption only.

A blueprint was proposed, with every number a hypothesis: one calibrated bank served at 20 items ("Glimpse"), 40 ("Profile") and 80 ("Fingerprint"), mixing fixed anchors, adaptive items, random items and repeats. Adaptive selection probably saves about half the questions, though that figure comes from general testing ([Kent thesis](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/93137)) and political evidence is thin. My Spearman-Brown arithmetic gives about 10 items per axis as a floor for reporting a score near .8 under generous assumptions. It should reveal coarse position, a personal mix of views, priorities, where someone is torn, which frames they refuse, and how structured they are. It cannot reveal identity, sincerity, future behavior or motive.

Three response options lose information. A single "pass" merges at least five states. The proposed set is agree or disagree with a strength tap, then **torn**, **don't care**, **don't know enough**, **wrong question**, plus a plain skip. No-opinion filters appear to suppress real views ([Krosnick et al.](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/the-impact-of-no-opinion-response-options-on-data-quality-non-att)), so these must be named choices modeled separately, never treated as the middle. The four-way split is untested. "Wrong question" is the option most native to this project, because refusals that cluster would flag an item that carries one camp's assumptions. Gelauff's finding on pass independently supports having it. Importance deserves a light separate layer. A small forced budget fights the habit of rating everything important, and quadratic voting surveys beat agreement scales at separating intense from weak preferences ([Cavaillé et al.](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:tse:iastwp:126569)).

### Forty answers are a key, not an anonymous record

This is the finding that should shape the design. No breach of a political quiz site was found, though absence of reports is weak evidence. The harm record comes from neighboring cases: an app that said it collected no identifiable data while collecting user IDs ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2019/12/ftc-issues-opinion-order-against-cambridge-analytica-deceiving-consumers-about-collection-facebook)), 7 of 11 Finnish voting-advice tools leaking political opinions to third parties ([abstract](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-981-97-1313-4_30)), an RNC contractor exposing data on nearly 200 million people with modeled views on 46 issues ([UpGuard](https://www.upguard.com/breaches/the-rnc-files)), and Prop 8 donors placed on a public map ([Next City](https://nextcity.org/daily/entry/mapping-proposition-8)). The nearest live US threat is legal compulsion: DHS reportedly sent hundreds of administrative subpoenas, with no judge reviewing them first, for the identities behind accounts critical of ICE ([NYT reprint](https://www.hawaiitribune-herald.com/2026/02/14/nation-world-news/homeland-security-wants-social-media-sites-to-expose-anti-ice-accounts/?nonamp=1)). How far companies complied is unclear.

A test on public Polis vote data, counting only aggregates and identifying no one, measured how many people had an answer pattern nobody else shared. Pass counted as an answer, so these are three-option answers.

| Conversation | People with all 40 | Unique at 10 items | at 20 | at 30 | at 40 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| march-on | 2,008 | 6.7% | 39.9% | 92.8% | 99.4% |
| vtaiwan.uberx | 349 | 47.9% | 90.1% | 93.8% | 94.6% |
| bowling-green | 332 | 46.5% | 85.5% | 92.6% | 96.4% |

Group sizes are small and small groups make uniqueness easier, but march-on has the largest group and the most lopsided answers, and it still reaches 99.4%. A simulation of 300,000 synthetic people on five-point items reached near-total uniqueness by 15 to 20 items, even with very strong opinion structure. Real populations clump more than that, and no population-scale study of attitude vectors exists. The practical conclusion holds anyway. Unique does not mean named. It means anyone holding some of a person's answers can find the rest, through someone who knows them, through predictions from their public posts, or through another quiz. **Trimming 40 items to 25 buys no safety.** Only going near 10 changes much, and that hurts placement. Finer answer scales and better measurement make a better key, so measurement quality and identifiability pull directly against each other. Item count should be set for placement, and protection has to come from not holding the vector.

A coarse label leaks far less. On a held-out test, guessing a person's other answers from their cluster beat a plain majority guess by about 6 to 9 points on contested data and about 0 on lopsided data. The label is still the harm on its own terms, and small clusters break it. Copying Community Notes' open data would be a serious error, because its files carry a permanent participant ID on every rating and each user's modeling group ([docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/twitter/communitynotes/main/documentation/under-the-hood/download-data.md)). A rating history is itself a fingerprint. A fingerprint is also close to enough to build a working model of someone: agents built from survey answers alone predicted held-out survey answers at 82%, near the 83% from two-hour interviews, normalized to each person's own consistency ([Park et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.10109)). Those were GSS items and not a 40-item set, but a stolen profile is a practice dummy for tailoring persuasion to that person.

The cheapest controls are the strongest: compute placement in the browser from published settings, save nothing by default, load no third-party scripts, keep no demographics beside answers, and never publish per-person data. Local differential privacy cannot build a map at small scale: at a per-item privacy level of 1, one pairwise correlation at ±0.01 needs about 219,000 people (calculated for this research). MACI is the wrong tool. Secure aggregation needs a second independent operator. Anonymous credentials are real but belong later.

The design is further along than the notes assumed. T0 visitors already react in the browser with placement computed client-side and "nothing stored server-side," and an exporter rule already sets aggregates only at k ≥ 50 (`docs/08-TECHNICAL-ARCHITECTURE.md` §7.3, §11.1). The trouble starts at T1.

### The red line collides with ratification, audits and the scorer

The research notes frame "the server never holds an answer vector" as a mostly cheap rule, with one conflict at spec step 6. Checking the documents shows the collision is wider. Reactions are stored in Postgres "while the account exists" with an email login (`docs/08-TECHNICAL-ARCHITECTURE.md` §11.2). D-31.3 says only people placed through real statement reactions can ratify, and a database trigger enforces that. OD-14 proposes eligibility of 12 or more non-pass reactions from at least three clusters' splitter sets. Inviting fidelity raters at random requires knowing who sits in the described cluster. The directional-shift audit compares users' reactions before and after agent contact, and the agent-free re-fit also needs them. The many-splits scoring recommended in the earlier report needs each rater's stance per idea family, which also comes from their votes. Each of these needs server-side reaction data.

A learned viewpoint solves less than it seems to. The notes correctly say matrix factorization can learn a viewpoint from ratings alone, so the scorer needs no fingerprint. That works for portability, where raters from other clusters ask whether a text is clarifying. **It does not work for fidelity**, because signing requires knowing who belongs to the described side, and a learned factor cannot say that. My reading is that a privacy-preserving version has its own cost. A cluster claim computed on the device and attested by the person is easier to fake than one the server verifies, so the sleeper attack gets cheaper unless zero-knowledge proofs are added, and those sit in the most expensive tier. In other words, the protection that best serves ordinary people weakens the guard on the project's central act. There is no free design here, which is why this needs Benjamin's choice and not an engineering default.

Pseudonyms do not rescue this either. Seasonal rotation suits the quarterly seasons in Spine §10.5, but the same section has reputation decaying on a 180-day half-life, and the spec wants public profiles. Long reputation needs stable identity while privacy needs rotation. The middle path is rotating each season and carrying over only a coarse tier, which the project can link at the moment of transfer. Long-run measurement also suffers: the planned retests and repeats only work if someone holds a person's earlier answers, so validation can happen only with a separately consented panel, or on the device.

### A type label risks making the tribes it describes

The evidence here is by analogy, and it deserves respect because it speaks to the project's name. A mailing that simply mentioned party registration raised identification with the latent party by 8.1 points ([Gerber, Huber and Washington](https://isps.yale.edu/research/publications/isps10-038)). Hacking's looping effect says classification changes the classified ([LRB](https://www.lrb.co.uk/the-paper/v28/n16/ian-hacking/making-up-people)). A UK study found voting-advice tools produced durable shifts in preference ([search summary](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/do-voting-advice-applications-have-durable-causal-effects-evidenc/)), which matters for a project that values non-convergence. No direct trial of a political type quiz was found. So the options are no team names, badges or share cards; "you answered like this school on these items and differ on these," never "you follow"; hidden names while answering; and a randomized label test before any public fingerprint.

Names matter because party loyalty can move people in either ideological direction, most among low-knowledge respondents ([Barber and Pope](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/abs/does-party-trump-ideology-disentangling-party-and-ideology-in-america/B5BAD0AE947BD3CF18D51D399263C8D3), search summary only). The "guess who said it" game in D17 reveals names during answering, so later answers in a session may be cued. That is my reasoning, and a randomized arm would measure it. One earlier caution should also be narrowed. Republicans who followed an opposing bot for a month moved more conservative ([Bail et al.](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1346854)), but that was unstructured elite messaging from a bot. It does not show that structured, disclosed, reciprocal dialogue backfires.

Any nudge built on a fingerprint should stay off by default. Eight proposed bounds exist in the notes: disclosed each time, started by the user, reasons and not weak spots, symmetric across clusters, no directional goal, on-device, quiet before elections, publishable. The EU ban on manipulation is narrower than people assume ([Art. 5](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/5/)), and the sleeper is Article 5(1)(c) on social scoring, which a public MOVE score used by an employer could resemble. **[Legal review]**

## BLOOM and the assembly stream leave a measurement gap, and a small role open

The people and links are now clear. BLOOM (bloom-project.org) is a nonprofit building a "Public Assembly on AI": up to 20 civic hosts, community assemblies from February to July 2027, then state assemblies, then a national civic assembly planned for 2028 ([announcement](https://bloomproject.substack.com/p/introducing-the-public-assembly-on)). Its assemblies are made up of people, with AI used only as tooling. The adviser's name is Audrey **Tang**, not Tong, and Lawfare calls Tang a senior adviser ([Lawfare](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/citizens--assemblies-in-ai-governance--an-interview-with-audrey-tang)). "AI citizen assemblies" is best read as citizens' assemblies about AI, helped by AI tools. Its Utah pilot produced 10 recommendations at 82% to 97% support, with BLOOM reporting 38 delegates and Lawfare 40 ([announcement](https://bloomproject.substack.com/p/introducing-the-public-assembly-on)). Each host must name a decision-maker and timeline, such as a county commission vote ([cohort page](https://bloom-project.org/cohort/)). That single rule answers the failure found across every case above, and it marks where Dialogos is weakest, since it feeds no decision.

At least six efforts use the name Declaration of Interdependence. The relevant one is Declare Interdependence / We the Interdependent, a public-writing process stewarded by Amelia Rose Barlow, who is a real public figure spelled as Benjamin recalled. Its page listed 47 submissions when fetched ([site](https://wetheinterdependent.com/declare-interdependence)). Tom Atlee reports an open-source model weaves these into a collective vision feeding a planned national citizens' assembly, without naming the model ([Atlee](https://tomatlee493488.substack.com/p/declaration-of-interdependence)). A separate Davos working declaration dated 22 January 2026 lists Project Interdependence as a partner ([site](https://www.interdependencedeclaration.org/)). The similarly named declareinterdependence.us is a different effort, and #Next250, the largest US one with 40+ organizations ([The Fulcrum](https://thefulcrum.us/democracy/bipartisan-civic-engagement)), has no link found to either.

**Declaring Our Tech Futures is the connector.** Its site names BLOOM and Declare Interdependence as early coalition partners, gives 2026 launch, 2027 local and state capacity, and a 2028 national citizens' assembly, and lists Barlow and Amanda Arch as co-stewards ([site](https://declaringourtechfutures.com/)). Tang is not on its adviser list. Barlow's essay asks for "a second draft" and a national assembly before the next presidential election ([Yahoo syndication of Rolling Stone](https://www.yahoo.com/news/politics/articles/father-gave-internet-declaration-independence-130000121.html)), and BLOOM's newsletter cites that call ([BLOOM](https://bloomproject.substack.com/p/whos-holding-the-steering-wheel)). "Works at BLOOM" would be wrong on current evidence. Nothing found says the two 2028 assemblies are the same event, and that is the most useful question to ask them. The partnership claim rests on the coalition's own page plus BLOOM's friendly citation.

The wider field mostly proposes and convenes. In-person assemblies with real delegates in 2026 are BLOOM's pilots and the UK's House of the People, 100 people selected by lot, stratified on initial attitudes to technology, with finals in Manchester 27 to 29 November ([press release](https://www.houseofthepeople.uk/press-release-worlds-first-grassroots-citizens-assembly-on-ai-launches)). The Citizens' Track proposes a global assembly of 100 to 500 at $1.5m to $3m and a federated database of outcomes ([report](https://citizens-track.org/2026/report/)). CIP runs recurring global polling ([Global Dialogues, search summary](https://globaldialogues.ai/)). A US, UK and UN stack is forming, with Clara Long and Richard Wilson's joint Guardian piece the visible bridge. No effort found uses "social contract" for a deliberative process, and "Second Draft" is the closest citizen-side phrase.

### Tang's conditions are the entry test for any role

Tang's stated line fits Dialogos's rules closely, with one sharp edge. First-pass clustering is fine if participants can see and reject the categories, and an AI-drafted bridging statement is fine if people then rate it. AI should not draft positions, decide which disagreements matter, or turn summaries into decisions, and "simulated personas are not a remedy for people who were never invited" ([Lawfare](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/citizens--assemblies-in-ai-governance--an-interview-with-audrey-tang)). "Agents never ratify" agrees. Clusters the person cannot reject do not, and a public agent leaderboard with a market on agent strategies would read badly in this crowd unless explained first. The strongest evidence for caution about AI writers cuts both ways: AI writers produce 52% of X's Helpful notes on 22% of ratings ([Miller et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.40067v1)), while 18 LLMs all scored minority opinions lower when summarizing ([Zhu et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05154v5)).

The gap is real, with one caveat. BLOOM's Open Poll maps agreement inside a community ([cohort page](https://bloom-project.org/cohort/)). Nobody found measures whether groups can describe each other in terms the other would sign. The caveat is that this search was by effort, not across the field for that measure. Note also that Dialogos's map is built from public figures, so it must not be used to pick delegates or stand in for a town until validated against real populations.

| Design critique | Ask Tang or the Oxford Civic AI group to critique the MOVE score against their principles | Tang's public conditions are testable | Outward-facing; exposes an unbuilt design |

| Pre-assembly landscape brief | Show Declaring Our Tech Futures which camps its coalition lacks | No named method for its 2028 question | Could read as one more advocate, not a neutral measure |

My reading of the deepest tension is that joining the stream both solves and threatens. Every success found had a route to power, and this stream is the nearest thing to one. But Chile's lesson is that a process owned by one cluster reproduces the divide, and this coalition looks like a cluster in its own right, civic and tech-reform in outlook. Which camps it lacks is untested. A measurement layer that joins it loses standing with the camps it is meant to measure, unless the coalition is mapped like everyone else. An offer limited to measurement and audit keeps more independence than co-branding. Pro-Human Declaration signatories, a strange-bedfellows coalition, could serve as an offline test with no outreach at all.

## Six corrections to the earlier report

The earlier report titled "Memetic mapping and computational democracy" stands in most respects. These change what it said or implied.

| # | The earlier report said | Correction | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Third novelty candidate: fidelity calibrated with real in-group statements and decoys, with no prior art found | **It shrinks.** In-group judges comparing real and imitated text is prior art: Collins and Evans's Imitation Game, used on gender, national identity, religiosity and sexuality, with a €2.26M ERC grant and a consumer app ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.730985/pdf)). Mixing in real comparators, reading at group level and signal-detection statistics are all prior art ([PMC8586539](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8586539)) | The earlier report credited only the Ideological Turing Test. What survives is narrow: engineered decoys, quote-anchored comparators from public figures, and a persistent directional cluster-pair score feeding reputation, with clusters from a different population's works. Decoys are searched thinly, so "not found" is weak. Collins et al. 2017 should be read before any novelty claim |
| 2 | Treated signing as Dialogos's meta-consensus measure, and called a same-fault-line view the "closest numeric stand-in" | **It measures reciprocal understanding, only one part of meta-consensus.** The concept has normative, epistemic and preference forms, and reciprocal understanding is implied by the normative form ([Dryzek and Niemeyer](https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/13db0edd-bc9e-45ab-ae40-9194d14bd78a/content)). A group can be accurately described and still judged illegitimate | "Signing score" or "reciprocal understanding score" is the honest name. `CLAUDE.md` defines meta-consensus as the narrower thing. This could replace the fixed 0.70 in OD-14. Decoys also carry an ethics cost: debrief people, and never attach a distorted statement to a named landmark |
| 4 | Top option: a fixed battery of 30 to 60 propositions read against all works | **It did not flag that the spec and D-31.4 rejected question-first scoring** | The option needs a decision and is narrower than presented. | 5 | "The people mapped are mostly not the raters," and "no literature solves that" | **There is a large relevant literature that went unsearched:** elite-public joint scaling, bridging items, differential item functioning. It does not solve this exact design, and it shows the problem is measurable and that shared items often fail | Jessee's check becomes a direct test, and a gap between thinkers and public is a measured form of `sense_fork` |
| 6 | Recommended clusters plus a residual factor plus averaging over many splits | **It did not count the privacy cost.** Many splits need rater stances held on a server | The evidence for the method stands, but it now competes with the proposed red line |

Smaller updates follow. Pew did publish after 2021, with a 2026 typology. Tensor methods have had a thin check and nothing justifies them. The Bail citation is narrowed as described above. The earlier suggestion of a survey question ("was this fair although your side did not win?") sits awkwardly with D16 and falls under that decision. The earlier report said no vault notes were consulted, and this round's social-contract note did read them. Candidates 1, 2, 4 and 5 from that report were not contradicted, and candidate 2 survives the new searches. All remain "untested combinations," not discoveries.

A prediction score could partner the signing score. Cluster A predicts what share of B would endorse held-out statements, scored against the overall rate so credit goes to knowing B specifically. High prediction with low signing suggests accurate contempt, and low prediction with high signing suggests flattery ([method basis](https://www.moreincommon.com/media/0fmblxb3/the-perception-gap.pdf)). The form is untested, and More in Common warns that question choice drives the gap. One cost is that it can only be computed with B's votes held somewhere, at least in aggregate.

OD-28 deserves a line of its own. It proposes that judge subagents gate the P3 self-recognition test because recruiting insiders is outward-facing. A model judging "they'd sign" is the third and weakest signature strength: predicted, not signed. That is acceptable for an internal gate as long as it is never reported as signing.

## Conclusion

The research started by asking whether these tools could be combined, and it ended somewhere else. They can be combined, and the combination is mostly old. What is new is an asymmetry in what should be visible: a person's answers are dangerous exactly because they are precise, and a signed description is valuable exactly because it is public. That reverses the usual civic-tech instinct to collect opinions and publish the summary. It suggests a project that holds very little about anyone, and publishes a growing record of who has understood whom.

The hard part is that the project's central act needs to know who belongs to a side, and that knowledge is the very thing most worth not holding. The cost of protecting people falls on the guard against fake signers, and no tool found dissolves that trade. Whether Dialogos joins the assembly stream is a smaller question than this one, because a measurement role is only as credible as the answer to it. A group that has reasoned through who may sign for a side, and what it refuses to know about them, would have something to offer that stream that nobody there has yet.
