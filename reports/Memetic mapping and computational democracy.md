# Measure What the Described Side Signs

Dialogos Arena's strongest opening is not a prettier map or a new scoring formula. It is a calibrated measure of whether a described group would sign its description. Combining Polis cluster matching, idea-based cluster matching and Community Notes bridging is mostly not novel: Polis and Community Notes are converging into one family, and votes have been joined to text in political science since at least 2014. The genuinely open ground is narrow, and "untested combination" is a fairer label than "discovery" until a targeted literature review confirms it. The most important candidate is scoring a description against the described group's own quote-anchored statements and deliberately distorted decoys, using a map built from a separate population's public works. A best-in-class map comes first from better data, which means reading every work against one fixed set of contested propositions, and second from honest drawing, which means a 2D-first map that shows its distortion and disputes on its face. The next research should cover open source, national security and Catholic social teaching, because those regions would most change the map. Our own work has four known issues and one open conflict in decision D-31.1, all set out below. Nothing already built was changed, and every recommendation here is an option for Benjamin's decision.

Turquoise Sound (she/her) supplied the references behind this report, and the credit for that starting point is hers. Her observation that adversarial narrative intelligence and Dialogos are close in form and opposite in purpose held up under inspection. Her exact wording on some points was unavailable, so where this report reconstructs an argument it says so. Most 2025 to 2026 papers cited here are preprints, not peer-reviewed work, and several were seen only as abstracts. Vendor numbers are the vendors' own. Judgments labeled "my reading" are inference.

## Four fields built one data shape for opposite purposes

### Strict memetics failed, and variant tracking survived it

Gene-like memetics has little scientific standing: critics hold that imitation is too error-prone, ideas lack traceable lineages and cultural traits are not discrete units ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/evolution-cultural/)). What survives works under other names. Leskovec and colleagues tracked mutating quoted phrases through about 90 million articles ([summary](https://archive.blogs.harvard.edu/mediaberkman/2010/02/16/jure-leskovec-on-meme-tracking-and-the-dynamics-of-the-news-cycle/)). Adamic and colleagues found the single result most useful to Dialogos: **subgroups pass along the variant that matches their beliefs or culture** ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1402.6792)). So which paraphrase a cluster keeps may be a cleaner fingerprint than which topics it discusses. Transmission-chain experiments add a way to measure decay as a message is relayed ([Mesoudi and Whiten](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2607337)). My reading is that "meme" remains a fine public word and a weak database unit. "Proposition lineage" and "variant" are safer, with lineage recorded as a probability. The word also carries military baggage: a 2006 Marine Corps study proposed a Meme Warfare Center ([Vice](https://www.vice.com/en_us/article/xyvwdk/meme-warfare)), and a 2016 NATO journal article argued for embracing memetic warfare ([StratCom COE](https://stratcomcoe.org/publications/its-time-to-embrace-memetic-warfare/164)).

The contemporary sensemaking scene offers good questions more than validated methods. The Open Memetics Institute is an informal network. Its useful ideas are "dark memetics" (influence the affected people cannot audit) and "no neutral culture scientist," but it has no published empirical results and its argument study rests on hand-picked screenshots ([OMI glossary](https://defenderofbasic.github.io/open-memetics-institute/glossary.html); [Human Memome Project](https://defenderofthebasic.substack.com/p/the-human-memome-project); [Anatomy](https://defenderofthebasic.substack.com/p/anatomy-of-an-internet-argument)). The concrete asset nearby is the Community Archive, which reports **26,179,220 opt-in tweets from 2,373 members** ([site](https://www.community-archive.org/)). It suits provenance testing for one scene and has two cautions: it contains replies to and mentions of people who never opted in, and Vitalik Buterin funds it while being a landmark in priority encounter PE-01. hyperstitions.com is a token market with no published team or audit ([site](https://www.hyperstitions.com/)), which shows why MOVE should never pay for a destination. Hyperstition is detectable only as a causal claim. Finance work on the Silicon Valley Bank run shows the design, using differences in exposure to link attention to deposit losses ([Cookson et al.](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4422754)). Dialogos can tag future-state claims as candidates, and should not label any as confirmed.

Three groups offer directly usable tools. The Meaning Alignment Institute's Moral Graph Elicitation trial reports that 89.1% of a representative sample of 500 Americans felt well represented, and about 89% found the result fair even when their own value lost ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.10636)). "Was this fair although your side did not win?" is a cheap survey question for MEET. Tom Murray's work measures social deliberative skill as a capacity rather than a developmental stage ([AIED 2013](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-1009/1001.pdf)), and reports a large effect from three simple tools: Story, Conversation Thermometer and Contribution Tags ([paper](https://web.cs.umass.edu/publication/docs/2013/UM-CS-2013-014.pdf)). The effect size itself was not retrieved. The Society Library maps claims and logical relations at scale, with 5,862 items from about 8,000 hours on one Diablo Canyon map ([Joyce](https://justjamiejoyce.substack.com/p/your-attempt-to-refute-argument-maps)), and in November 2025 it announced work on multiple epistemological paradigms ([updates](https://www.societylibrary.org/society-library-updates)).

The "Platonic" critique of that approach could not be found in print, so what follows is a reconstruction, not Turquoise Sound's words. A single logical object behind many phrasings ignores who gets to speak, what goes unsaid and how groups read the same words differently. **The critique lands on Dialogos too**, because its neutral-proposition step is the same move. The fair framing is that the Library is a complement. Dialogos adds clusters and sign-off by the described side. Hall's point that decoding need not follow encoding ([summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encoding/decoding_model_of_communication)) suggests one candidate measure: per-cluster reading positions on the same proposition.

### The narrative-intelligence market sells a threat console

Blackbird.AI is the clearest commercial case. Its pipeline groups identical claims into "narrative objects," each one claim with a shared stance, then adds bot, manipulation and cohort signals and ranks the results ([Blackbird](https://blackbird.ai/blog/blackbird-ai-turns-millions-of-posts-into-actionable-narrative-intelligence/)). AWS lists 12-month packages from **$54,800 to $299,000** ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/marketplace/pp/prodview-7dyxpokn2tuhg)). Its definitions conflict: the glossary ties an attack to misinformation tactics ([glossary](https://blackbird.ai/glossary-of-terms/)), while another page calls it "any assertion that can cause harm by shaping perceptions" ([FAQ](https://blackbird.ai/narrative-attacks/)). Its most revealing artifact is a [marketing mockup](https://blackbird.ai/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/narrative-feed-3.jpg) about Boeing, where the source is SEC filings and bot, anomaly and toxicity signals all read Low, yet priority reads High. My reading is that priority there tracks reputational harm to the client, not manipulation, though this is one mockup, not a measured pattern. No formula, accuracy figure or independent evaluation was found.

Blackbird's narrative object is nearly the same data type as a Dialogos proposition with stance. The difference is governance: who pays, who is centered and who may dispute. Across the field the shape repeats as actors, content, claim clusters and communities over time, shown as force maps, ranked feeds and scorecards. Graphika sells targeting and manipulation detection from one product line ([Graphika](https://graphika.com/use-cases/audience-and-influence-mapping)). Zignal Labs moved from PR to a **$5.7M ICE contract** ([The Lever](https://www.levernews.com/ice-just-bought-a-social-media-surveillance-botice-just-bought-a-social-media-surveillance-bot/)). The common failure is secrecy. Hamilton 68's hidden account list became the story ([summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamilton_68)), and Faktisk.no found real people among profiles Cyabra called fake, with methods undisclosed ([Faktisk.no](https://www.faktisk.no/artikkel/israeli-ai-firm-claims-fake-profiles-targeted-norwegian-pm-investigation-finds-several-were-real/127272)).

"Narrative" itself has no agreed definition, and Piper and colleagues treat it as a matter of degree ([Piper et al.](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10342552)). It can mean a repeated claim, a who-did-what-to-whom structure ([relatio](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.01720v3.pdf)), an actant network ([Tangherlini et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.09961)) or an event sequence ([survey](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2302.08351)). Most commercial "narratives" are the first of these. Dialogos should say which it means, since a labeled ladder from proposition to framework stops the interface implying structure that was never extracted.

Effects evidence calls for modest language. Exposure to Russia's 2016 Twitter campaign showed no meaningful link to attitudes or voting ([Eady et al.](https://doaj.org/article/ae23fc2e267d4fe7beeeb88109b0b65a)), and evidence for Cambridge Analytica's persuasion is thin ([NBC](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/politics-news/cambridge-analytica-s-effectiveness-called-question-despite-alleged-facebook-data-n858256)). Legal and political cost is real all the same. SPLC paid **$3.375M** over a list entry ([Religion Clause](https://religionclause.blogspot.com/2018/06/splc-pays-3375m-for-wrongful-inclusion.html)), and *Hines v. Stamos* was reportedly dismissed after three years of cost, by a defendant's own account ([DiResta](https://www.reneediresta.com/happy-july-4-we-beat-stephen-miller-in-court/)). Joan Donovan's life-cycle model adds the observer to the system: a campaign moves through planning, seeding, responses, mitigation and adaptation ([Casebook](https://mediamanipulation.org/methods)). The map should log its own effects at the "responses" stage. It should not apply a manipulation lens to sincere worldviews.

### Computational sociology asks a better question than "who agrees"

Belief-structure research asks who organizes beliefs the same way. Correlational Class Analysis treats two people as sharing a schema **even when their attitudes are exact inverses** ([Boutyline](https://www.sociologicalscience.com/download/vol-4/may/SocSci_v4_353to393.pdf)). That idea matters most here: under our cosine distance, opposites sit at maximum distance, while CCA would read e/acc and d/acc as possibly arguing over one axis. DellaPosta finds polarization grows when alignments spread across more topics ([ASR](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/pluralistic-collapse-the-oil-spill-model-of-mass-opinion-polariza/)). The catch is scale. These methods assume dense tables and many respondents, and the Relational Class Analysis page says it is not designed for missing data ([Goldberg](https://web.stanford.edu/~amirgo/rca.html)). Our data has roughly 27 thinkers, 360 ideas and 10% fill, so about 2.7 holders per idea by my estimate. The concepts transfer. The estimators mostly do not.

Our method is close to Discourse Network Analysis, whose statement of actor, concept and agreement is effectively our holding. Leifeld warns that active actors can look central and hide coalitions, and that coding one issue produces two coalitions "by design" ([Leifeld](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/121525/1/121525.pdf)). A coalition also implies coordination ([Weible and Nohrstedt](https://is.muni.cz/el/fss/podzim2021/MEBn4001/um/povinna_literatura/Weible_Nohrstedt_2012.txt)), so "schools" is the honest word until appearance or citation data shows otherwise.

On LLM measurement the evidence points one way. Asked a narrow question about supplied text, LLMs can do well: averaged positions reached r > .90 against benchmarks ([Le Mens and Gallego](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.16639v3)). Stance remains hard, with a best weighted F1 of .73 on German news and human agreement averaging only .55 ([Walker and Angst](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12629487)). Our own run agrees: only 80 of 734 machine-proposed holdings survived blind reading (`docs/adr/ADR-035-idea-families-and-two-reader-structure.md`). Labels at 80 to 90% accuracy still bias estimates unless a small gold sample corrects them ([Egami et al.](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/d862f7f5445255090de13b825b880d59-Abstract.html)). LLM imputation also did worse for low-status, minority and non-partisan people ([Kim and Lee](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.09620v3)). Simulated people should stay out of placement.

### The Habermas Machine shows acceptable text, not mutual understanding

Tessler and colleagues trained a generator and a personal ranking model, then picked a winner by Schulze election, in groups of about five ([Science, full-text mirror](https://www.rivista.ai/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/science.adq2852.pdf)). People preferred its statements to a human mediator's by 56% to 44%, and group agreement rose by roughly 8 points. "Group agreement" there is the gap between sides on a position question, so **it measures convergence, which Dialogos rejects as a success metric**. Three further findings change the reading. Similar convergence appeared on rounds where the human mediator won, which suggests the format matters more than the AI. External judges saw no significant difference on fair summary. Minority weight rose in the text, yet opinions overall shifted toward the majority.

Critics press exactly this gap. Flechtner argues the validation cannot tell reasoning apart from a framing that happens to be acceptable ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.10186)). The supporting data comes from LLM-only groups, so extending it to the Machine is an argument, not a finding. Oleart and Palomo call it "deliberation without citizens" ([JDD](https://delibdemjournal.org/article/id/1839/)). The authors themselves conceded "minimally deliberative" and floated "we all agree that we cannot agree" as a valid output ([Burnes talk](https://www.youtube.com/live/-cCguFRH7f4)). That last remark is the closest they come to Dialogos's aim. No independent replication or published field deployment was found.

Recent controlled tests of LLM facilitation are mostly null on outcomes: 281 groups showed no change in decision accuracy ([ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3816907)), and 4,088 US adults showed no fall in polarization ([DeliberationBench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.10018)). Separately, 18 LLMs all scored minority opinions lower when summarizing, by about 0.036 on a 0 to 1 scale ([Zhu et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05154v5)). The pattern suggests scoring outcomes, not satisfaction. Process scores offer little help, since LLM-only groups matched humans on automated discourse quality ([Flechtner](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.10186)).

Terminology needs care. Dryzek and Niemeyer define meta-consensus as agreement on the legitimacy of values, the credibility of beliefs and the structure of choices, and it "implies reciprocal understanding" ([AJPS 2006](https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/13db0edd-bc9e-45ab-ae40-9194d14bd78a/content)). The Habermas team uses the same word for accepting an outcome as legitimate because the process was fair ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.05904)). Dialogos means something narrower and more checkable: a signed claim about a specific text. The nearest instrument is the Ideological Turing Test, where a person passes if opponents agree with their argument at least as much as with real proponents' arguments, tested on 1,200 UK participants ([Brand et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12519043/)). The Deliberative Reason Index and Q method are useful periodic audits, not per-text scores.

### Polis and Community Notes are becoming one family, with measured weaknesses

Polis fills missing votes with column averages, projects to 2D, groups people by k-means and scores statements against that one grouping. Its own code comment calls the imputation a weakness ([pca.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/compdemocracy/polis/edge/delphi/polismath/pca_kmeans_rep/pca.py)). Gelauff's study of 119 conversations finds that **74.6% report two groups, but recomputing from final votes drops that to 62.5%, the two agree in only 70 of 112 cases, and exported clusters cannot be reproduced** ([Gelauff](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.28368)). Polis's group count is largely a platform output, so a two-group result is not evidence of two camps. Blair and colleagues show that group-informed consensus turns negatively correlated with the true ranking at low observation rates, and the median Polis observation rate is 15.5%, because all the sensitivity sits in one clustering step ([Blair et al.](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~nisarg/papers/bridging.pdf)).

Community Notes splits each rating into a general mean, the rater's habit, the note's quality and a one-dimensional viewpoint fit ([ranking notes](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/twitter/communitynotes/main/documentation/under-the-hood/ranking-notes.md)). A note publishes at an intercept of at least 0.40 with low viewpoint spread. The strongest 2025 to 2026 results are all preprints, and four matter most:

| Finding | Number | Source |
|---|---|---|
| A second axis is real | Held-out error 0.3085 at one factor, 0.2899 at two, 0.2892 at three. Notes barely splitting axis one publish at 71.5% near the second-axis origin, 11.7% far from it | [Andreou and Sirivianos](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.21496) |
| Helpful notes are suppressed even with honest raters | Over 40% in simulation through X's code. About 20% coordinated raters can fully suppress a targeted group | [Truong et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.02615) |
| The bare core model can be gamed cheaply | Up to 10.7% of below-median notes cross the bar for about $30.50. This attacks the bare model, not X's production guards | [Selvam et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.01824v1) |
| AI writers now do much of the work | X's Community Writer yields 52% of Helpful notes on 22% of ratings. Humans remain the final raters | [Miller et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.40067v1) |

X has wrapped the core in a stack of guards, and since November 2025 added a Gaussian model that approximates a geometric mean of helpfulness across the viewpoint axis ([ranking notes](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/twitter/communitynotes/main/documentation/under-the-hood/ranking-notes.md)). I read that as a soft version of Polis's product across groups. The Community Notes intercept, Polis's product, Remesh's minimum and COCM's pair sum all answer one question: how much approval survives across a chosen structure of difference? Blair's key result is that metrics tied to one fixed partition are structurally weak, and that averaging over the split each statement induces is more robust. Field evidence for bridging stays modest. Consensus-seeking selection can flatten conflict and erase minorities ([Hafid et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.15439)), and upranking bridging alone was not significant in the Prosocial Ranking Challenge ([Stray et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.19626)).

## A best-in-class map needs better data first and honest drawing second

### Describing tribes accurately means comparing people on the same questions

No published method rescues 10% fill by clever analysis. The largest gain would come from changing the data, so thinkers are compared on the same items rather than on whatever each happened to mention. Options below follow my ranking from the notes, and none has been tested on our data.

| Option | In plain words | Why consider it | Effort |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed battery of 30 to 60 contested propositions, read against all 93 works, with accepts, rejects or no stated position, plus a quote | Everyone answers the same exam | Attacks the 10% fill directly. The list is itself a political choice, so it could be drafted from the map's own attack links and published | Medium |
| Distance only over cells both people filled, reporting overlap | Compare two people only where both spoke | Matches ADR-035's rule that silence is missing | Low |
| A "same fault line" view | Show pairs who oppose each other on one shared axis | Closest numeric stand-in for meta-consensus, though unvalidated for this use | Low to medium |
| Held-out stance prediction and test-retest across works as headline validity | Can the map predict a position it was not shown? | Silhouette against curated niches rewards agreeing with the curators | Medium |
| Sense comparison inside one idea family | Compare how two schools use a word on the same topic | The current fork test can fire because schools discuss different things | Low to medium |
| Specific MCA with silence as a passive category | A true shared space for people and ideas | The ADR-035 failure was on single ideas, so it says nothing about family level. Behavior at this size is unknown | Medium |
| Human coding of a random sample, with Krippendorff's alpha | Check the machine readers with people | Two model readers are not independent evidence | Medium |

Accuracy also needs validation with independent errors, since model readers, embeddings and imputation share biases. Co-citation, co-appearance and human coding do not. Hennig's bootstrap gives a usable scale, where mean per-cluster stability below .6 should not be trusted ([clusterboot](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/fpc/html/clusterboot.html)). Multiverse analysis would show how much our chosen settings drive the nine schools ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.19745)). With about three people per school, one person moving shifts the result, so the silhouette gap reported in the notes (+0.09 for our structure against −0.04 for text embeddings, values not stored in the repo) deserves an interval. Self-identification and holder ratification remain the true test, and they need outreach only Benjamin can approve. D-31.14 already says definitions should come from the people described.

Outside evidence limits how far any thinker map generalizes. All 4.7 million AI tweets from January 2023 sorted into communities of use such as art, crypto and data science, with "no community that discussed AI specifically" ([Kaiser and Green](https://firstmonday.org/ojs/index.php/fm/article/download/14436/12423)). The Atlas camps are therefore an elite structure, not a public one. Pew and Hidden Tribes map publics from answers ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2021/11/09/beyond-red-vs-blue-the-political-typology-2/); [Hidden Tribes](https://hiddentribes.us/)), while the Atlas maps elites from reasons. Its one real advantage over every typology reviewed is that a person can check a sentence attributed to them.

### The visual should show its losses and disputes on the surface

Task should choose the projection. A review of 136 papers assigns neighbor and cluster-finding to t-SNE and UMAP, and point and cluster distance to global methods like MDS ([Jeon et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.08725v2)). The Atlas invites "how far is A from B?", so MDS is the right family. Moving to UMAP would improve the look and weaken those readings. The two papers on t-SNE and UMAP distortion conflict on framing ([Chari and Pachter](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1011288); [Lause et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11446450)), and the defensible reading is that distances are not preserved while neighborhoods partly are.

On a flat screen, 3D rarely pays. Across 816 scatterplots, 2D usually sufficed ([Sedlmair et al.](https://www.tableau.com/research/publications/empirical-guidance-scatterplot-and-dimension-reduction-technique-choices)). For finding points, dots beat landscapes and 2D landscapes beat 3D ones ([Tory et al.](https://manuscript.isc.ac/Inventory/49/964290.htm)). The landscape metaphor is not self-evident to lay users ([Fabrikant et al.](https://www.zora.uzh.ch/entities/publication/327fbcdd-5bc3-4378-8c44-20e8a05f1518)). The Sedlmair and Tory results were read at abstract level, and Tory limits the result to tasks about the points. No study tests terrain for remembering the shape of a cultural field, so Benjamin's terrain idea stays open as an experiment. The evidence against it is narrower than it first looks.

The Atlas's fixed height by layer is honest because it is a labeled category and not a fitted axis. The real problem is that links pull nodes off their positions, so distance mixes two meanings while viewers read near as similar. Exemplars such as DataMapPlot, Polis and Talk to the City put understanding in labels and linked summaries more than in geometry ([DataMapPlot](https://datamapplot.readthedocs.io/en/latest/intro_splash.html); [Talk to the City](https://ai.objectives.institute/talk-to-the-city)). What Benjamin likes in Blackbird is plausibly the narrative as a named object with a card, though that is an inference and it helps to know which screen he means. I found nothing that plots depth of belief as a measured axis from data, so the layer axis is potentially new and also unvalidated.

| Rank | Option | What it adds | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Fixed-position 2D map with layer selector and cards | Evidence-backed base, lowest cost | Less striking than current 3D |
| 2 | Distortion lens | Select a mark and see its true full-space neighbors as links | Must be written plainly: "these two only look close" |
| 3 | Fault cross-section between two clusters | Shows shared ground, forks and cruxes by layer, the new object nobody else has | May imply deeper means truer |
| 4 | Signed map with points of view | Default map plus cluster-authored views, as [Natural Earth](https://www.naturalearthdata.com/blog/admin-0-countries-point-of-views/) does for borders. Ratified areas solid, disputed hatched | Outward-facing, so needs Benjamin's approval |
| 5 | Significance-tested alluvial of schools | Draws only splits and merges that survive resampling ([Rosvall and Bergstrom](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0008694)) | Too little time depth now |
| 6 | Small multiples and a school tree | Static accuracy beats animation for analysis ([Robertson et al.](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/effectiveness-of-animation-in-trend-visualization/)). A tree needs no projection | Many panels on phones |
| Test only | Terrain, and 3D as "explorer mode" | Memorable | Readers invent meaning, such as a basin reading as "bad" |

Model uncertainty and social contest deserve separate marks, or a disputed placement will look like a statistical accident. Almost none of these forms has been tested with users, so a small comparison of points, terrain and 3D across cluster readers would itself be original work.

Because a public worldview map is also a targeting list, these options deserve equal weight with the visuals. The strongest structural safeguard is that nobody can load "my brand" or "my opponent" as the center. Others include "described" and never "detected," no words like threat or extremist on the public map, soft multi-cluster placement, a published landmark list and method, no bulk export of person-to-cluster data, a red-team exercise, and a study of the map's own effects, since opposing exposure backfired for Republicans in Bail's study ([Nieman Lab](https://www.niemanlab.org/2018/08/republicans-who-follow-liberal-twitter-bots-actually-become-more-conservative/)). Legal counsel and possible insurance before launch have a real cost, so that decision is Benjamin's. This report is not legal advice.

## Five problems in our own work are cheap to settle now

Each issue was rechecked against the repo for this report, and none has been changed.

**Silence handling does not match ADR-035.** ADR-035 says "Silence is missing data." In `packages/arena_map/src/arena_map/structure.py`, `person_space` starts from a table of zeros and subtracts each family's mean. A silent person therefore gets the value minus-the-mean. Two silent people look alike on that family, and a person silent on a widely accepted family reads as mild dissent. Someone who both accepts and rejects within a family can also cancel to zero, indistinguishable from silence. The size of the effect is unknown. Options are to leave it and disclose it, to mask silent cells in a side-by-side test of school stability, to keep accept and reject counts separately, or to try a two-part model of whether a person addressed a family and then what stance they took. The two-part model fits best in principle, because thinkers choose what to discuss, so silence is informative ([Rosas et al.](https://macmillan.yale.edu/sites/default/files/files/resources/papers/rosas_etal_2009.pdf)).

**The 54% figure does not describe what is drawn.** `scripts/build_atlas.py` computes `three_axes_share` as the first three components of the person-by-family table, and the page says "Three axes hold" that share. The drawn map is 2D MDS on cosine distance plus a height fixed by layer, and height uses none of those three axes. So 54% is a property of the full data, not of the picture, and the "46% lost" reading in the visualization notes does not hold. The share kept by the 2D plane is unmeasured. The value itself appears only on the generated page, which is not stored in the repo, so I traced the formula and could not confirm the number. A related detail: the neighborhood figure uses sklearn's `trustworthiness`, whose docstring in our code describes the opposite direction, and it compares Euclidean full-space distance while the map uses cosine. Options are to reword the line, to report a measure of the drawn plane, or to drop the number until the layout settles.

**Karp is missing from the seed.** A case-insensitive search of `seed/SEED-v4.json` finds no match, yet Karp is the Atlas's 27th person and sits in no named school. Options are to add the entry or to record why the Atlas includes someone outside the seed. `apps/atlas/copy.json` also describes Karp's dissertation in stronger terms than the one source reached, which confirms the jargon-and-aggression theme ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alex_Karp)). That sentence is worth checking against the thesis.

**The docs are wrong on Habermas Machine code.** `docs/research/B2-computational-democracy.md` says no public code was found at line 169, repeats it in a table at line 301, and `docs/research/BIBLIOGRAPHY.md` repeats it at line 145. ### D-31.1 still carries an open conflict about clusters as the viewpoint dimension

D-31.1 says the "viewpoint dimension is the map's real clusters, not a single inferred left/right axis." The conflict is between that sentence and the evidence, and it has four parts. First, a fixed partition is exactly what Blair shows to be structurally weak. Second, a hidden axis can suppress notes, as Andreou and Sirivianos show, and nine schools are an unsettled choice, since ADR-035 calls nine the top of the tested range and "open." Third, `docs/08-TECHNICAL-ARCHITECTURE.md` §4.12.2 gives every cluster equal weight regardless of size, so **splitting a cluster doubles its voice and boundaries become political**. Fourth, D-31.1 says "the map's real clusters" without saying which. The Atlas schools come from 27 thinkers' public works, while §4.12.2 places raters by their reactions. These are different objects built from different people.

One correction to the research notes matters here. The Polis note says D-31.1 leaves the form open and recommends a hybrid with a free residual factor. Architecture §4.12.2 already specifies that hybrid: cluster offsets plus a weak residual factor at three times Community Notes' penalty, with fidelity raters mostly randomly invited and self-selected votes capped at 50%. So the formula question is further along than the note implies. What remains open is the role and governance of that design, which the decision log does not state. Under project precedence the log outranks the Architecture, so the gap should be resolved and not silently chosen.

Three pressures in the design also deserve Benjamin's attention. Round 2 removes raters below 0.66 helpfulness, which rewards matching outcomes. Applied to fidelity, that risks rewarding conformity inside a cluster, and for a project named after schismogenesis the instrument could harden the divide it measures. The fidelity rule of F ≥ 0.70 has no baseline, so it cannot tell a faithful description from a flattering one, which OD-14 could address before its 2026-12-06 date. There are also circular loops: statements shape votes, votes shape membership, and membership shapes who ratifies. The scorer has not been built, since `packages/arena_reputation` holds only an empty `__init__.py`, so changing these now costs little.

| Decision for Benjamin | Options | My recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| What "viewpoint dimension" means in D-31.1 | (a) Clusters alone; (b) clusters plus a residual factor, per §4.12.2; (c) (b) plus averaging over many splits | (c), with the residual reported as a standing audit of what the map misses |
| Which clusters count | Atlas schools, reaction-placed clusters, or both with a stated link | Name one explicitly, and report results with and without cluster terms |
| Who counts equally | Per cluster, per rater, or sensitivity reporting across cluster counts | Keep per-cluster and publish how results move as boundaries change |
| Fidelity bar in OD-14 | Fixed 0.70, or calibrated against in-group statements and decoys | Pilot calibration, since it needs people and his approval |
| Rater reputation | Agreement with outcomes, or informant reliability via cultural consensus theory | Test the second offline. It also rewards fit, so it reduces the risk without removing it |
| Keep the map independent | Allow feedback, or compute the map only from public works, frozen per round | Keep it independent, since this breaks the main loop |

## The combination is mostly old, but one narrow claim looks new

The direct answer to the novelty question is that the machinery is established and a specific use of it is open. Votes and text were jointly modeled by Lauderdale and Clark in 2014 ([AJPS](https://www.tomclarkphd.com/files/LauderdaleClarkAJPS2014.pdf)) and Kim, Londregan and Ratkovic in 2018 ([Political Analysis](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/C580ED354853271EFA759E289DC73C3A/S1047198718000074a.pdf/estimating_spatial_preferences_from_votes_and_text.pdf)). Polis 2.0 already uses language models for topics and statements while keeping vote math separate ([PR #3199](https://github.com/compdemocracy/polis/pull/3199)). Pre-specified groups are ordinary in Remesh. The closest text-to-score model was built as an attack ([Selvam et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.01824v1)). Dialogos should not describe joint votes-and-text scoring as new.

Off-the-shelf embeddings also fail the idea-based half: they score near chance when a text copies an anchor's words with the stance flipped, though per-topic tuning reaches 77.6% on natural data ([Blair, Procaccia and Tambe](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2605.08360)). That confirms Benjamin's worry that embeddings cluster by topic.

Four designs were assessed. They are layers, not rivals, and the ratings below are reasoned rather than measured.

| Design | Novel? | Attack risk | Circularity | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. Seed Polis-style statements from extracted ideas | No | Low to moderate | Moderate: only axes in the seeds can appear | Reasonable, with a share of statements from participants |
| B. One joint model of thinkers, participants and ratings | No in general; the two-population form is uncommon | High | High | Offline research only. About 27 thinkers cannot support it, and `sense_fork` exists because groups use words differently |
| C. Clusters as sole viewpoint | Partly | High: boundaries, sleepers | High | Avoid as the sole structure |
| C-hybrid plus many splits | Candidate | Moderate | Lower | Prototype. Close to §4.12.2 already, with splits still to add |
| D. Described cluster signs, calibrated | Strongest candidate | Moderate | Moderate | Prototype first |

Five combinations turned up no prior art in the searches run: a live bridging structure derived from a separate population's public works; a persistent, directional, cluster-pair score for "the described side signs"; fidelity calibrated with real in-group statements and decoys in a live system; a residual factor used as a standing audit of a supplied map; and splits defined by idea families, one step past Blair's suggestion of weighting splits by substance. Cultural consensus theory, which estimates a group's answer key and reliable informants with no outside truth, also appears unused in bridging or reputation systems ([Anders et al.](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~zoravecz/bayes/data/Articles/ANDORABAT14_MC-CRM.pdf)). Each is a small step on an existing line, not a new field.

The third candidate deserves the most attention. Mix each description, blind to author, with real quote-anchored statements from that cluster's landmarks and with distorted decoys. Count it as signed only if it scores as well as the real statements and beats the decoys. This extends the Ideological Turing Test's passing rule and uses the map's quote anchors directly, and that is why the map and scorer belong together. Separate rating wording is essential: "this is what we think" must differ from "this is true," or a cluster can refuse accurate descriptions it finds uncomfortable. A description any cluster would sign says nothing, so distinctiveness needs its own check.

The steelman against this claim is serious. Meta-consensus has a mature literature, the Deliberative Reason Index exists, and no search was run on meta-consensus scoring specifically, which makes it the likeliest place for overlooked prior art. The people mapped are mostly not the raters, so a rater near a school is only a proxy for it, and no literature solves that. Nine schools also make 72 directed pairs. Even X sees 54% of unresolved notes under 10 ratings, so rater supply will bind first.

Four offline experiments could test this at no cost, though publishing any result would need Benjamin's approval: compare Community Notes-style factoring, Polis consensus and Blair's measures on public Polis exports (no direct benchmark was found); mask silence in `person_space` and compare stability; simulate sleeper and cluster-splitting attacks before writing the scorer; and, with his approval, pilot calibrated fidelity with a small invited group.

## Conclusion

Every field reviewed here tripped on the same thing: what it measures is easier to get than what it wants. Polis measures its own grouping, the Habermas Machine measures acceptability, vendors measure risk to a client, and Community Notes measures survival across one axis. Dialogos's bet is a harder quantity, and its existing quote anchors are the unusual asset that makes that quantity calibratable. That reframes the relation between the layers: the map's main job may be to supply the real statements against which descriptions are tested, not to serve as a picture or a scoring axis.

The largest risk is the name of the project. Giving clusters power over ratification and reputation could manufacture the divides the map describes, and that is the failure the design should be built to detect, since the scorer does not yet exist. The most valuable next step is therefore a decision and not more code: what D-31.1's clusters are for, and who gets to decide their boundaries.
